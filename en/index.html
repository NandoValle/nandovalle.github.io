I
The anomaly

They buried Paganini nine times.

Thirty-six years without a final grave.

Niccolò Paganini died in Nice on 27 May 1840. The bishop refused him consecrated ground. The body was embalmed. It moved from place to place for decades. Only in 1876 did the definitive authorisation for burial arrive.

Thirty-six years is not a detail.

And it all begins with a refusal made by Paganini himself.

Rule of this pageWhere there is a document, a document. Where there is interpretation, interpretation. Where there is doubt, doubt.
II
The refusal

He thought he was not dying yet.

In May 1840, already gravely ill, Paganini received a priest sent to give him the last rites.

He refused. Not because he was proclaiming some dark faith. He simply thought he was not dying yet.

A week later he was dead of an internal haemorrhage, with almost no voice left. Father Caffarelli reported to the bishop that the violinist intended to refuse the sacraments. On the strength of that report, the Church refused burial in consecrated ground.

That explains the administrative decision. It does not explain the ground it fell on.

Because, before dying, Paganini already carried another name.

Next pieceThe historical fact is not that the Devil was there. It is that there were people convinced he was.
III
The name they gave it

Devil.

What matters here is not whether anyone really saw anything. The historical fact is that they believed they saw.

In Vienna, a spectator claimed to have seen the Devil standing beside Paganini, guiding his bow. Others swore they could make out hooves under his clothes.

The extraordinary thing is not the legend. It is the need for it.

Faced with a technique that seemed physically impossible, the audience looked for a word able to explain it. It found a very old one.

The audience used the word it had.

Seventy years later, in a poem about initiation, Aleister Crowley stated a similar mechanism. The speaker is Marsyas — the satyr who, in the myth, was flayed alive after a musical contest with Apollo:

“So the adept is called insane
For his frank failure to explain.”

Source: Aleister Crowley, Aha!, The Equinox I (3), 1910, printed p. 30. Marsyas speaking.

And, a few lines further on:

“Some fool thinks names are things.”

Hypothesis of the essayWhen technique outruns the vocabulary of the people watching, imagination fills the distance.
IV
The body

The hand explained part of the prodigy.

A doctor who examined Paganini recorded something strange: his hand was not particularly large. It was extraordinarily flexible.

The joints moved in unusual ways. The fingers reached positions that seemed incompatible with a normal hand.

Today we can discuss medical hypotheses. In a nineteenth-century audience, nobody had that vocabulary.

The same body that widened the limits of technique also charged another price. From 1823 on, Paganini was treated with mercury in enormous doses, combined with opium. He lost every tooth; the jaw deteriorated. By July 1839 he had not a single tooth left and could barely eat. Modern analysis of his hair found mercury in the strands.

The hand explained the technique.
The treatment explained part of the ruin.
Neither of them explained the legend.

A necessary separationThe text does not turn a medical hypothesis into a retroactive diagnosis.
V
The denial

Paganini confirmed no pact.

He did the opposite.

He denied it in writing. More than once. He tried to use documents against what the audience had already decided to see.

But he went on appearing dressed in black. Pale. Gaunt. Hair dishevelled. Long coat. Few words.

On paper, he denied it.
In person, he let the image survive.

Perhaps because an explanation closes what mystery keeps alive.

Not to be confusedA public image is not a confession. Atmosphere is not evidence.
VI
The hidden instrument

Paganini played the guitar.

Not as a curiosity.

Between 1801 and 1804 he studied the instrument seriously. It was no hobby: 43 Ghiribizzi, 37 sonatas and 24 further pieces for solo guitar — a decomposable count of 104 works. Almost all of his chamber music was written with the guitar inside it.

But he almost never performed on it in public. The violin belonged to the stage. The guitar stayed close to the private circle.

One of them built the legend. The other stayed almost hidden inside it.

There is even a physical object running through this story: in 1838, Vuillaume lent him a guitar by Grobert, of Mirecourt. Paganini signed the soundboard; after his death, Hector Berlioz signed the other side. The instrument is today at the Musée de la Musique in Paris, catalogued as item E.375.

The man died.
The sound went on.

VII
Who went and read

The story only reaches the guitar because somebody went after the documents.

Nando Valle is a lawyer and a guitarist. He read about the nine burials, found the number strange, and did what one does with a strange case file: he went after the sources. The six chapters above are what he found.

There is a personal reason the subject felt familiar. In 1993 and 1994, from sixteen to eighteen years old, he read several books by Éliphas Lévi and Aleister Crowley. He began with Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie; in 1994 he reached The Book of the Law. He says that, that year, he used to joke to whoever was around that he was a magician.

There is no independent verification possible for this. It is a personal account — and it stays marked as a personal account.

On the other side of the Atlantic, in 1993, Alan Moore turned forty and announced he had decided to become a magician. He took up the worship of Glycon, a snake-god whose material fraud had been known since antiquity. Moore knows this: for him, exposing the puppet does not remove the god; it merely moves the address to the imagination.

Valle says he had no idea who Alan Moore was.

Thirty-two years went by.
What remained was a doubt.

He remembered a passage about keys. And he remembered seven or eight, without ever being able to settle which of the two counts was right.

Rule of verifiabilityAn autobiography does not become an external fact just because it is in print. Here, what only Nando can report stays marked as his report.
VIII · the keys · Aha! · 1910

Seven. Eight. One.

He went to check. In March 1910, Crowley published in The Equinox a dialogue-poem called Aha!. The title page announces it as “the sevenfold mystery of the ineffable love” and promises to set out the path of initiation from beginning to end, in the dialogue between Marsyas, the adept, and Olympas, his disciple.

“There are seven keys to the great gate,
being eight in one and one in eight.”

Seven keys to the great gate — being eight in one and one in eight. The doubt carried for thirty-two years was not necessarily a failure of memory: both counts are in the same couplet.

02

03

04

05

Free thought from the senses and watch it.

06

Fix the mind on one thought; “burn up the being in a word”.

07

Let not even “God” distract attention from the chosen act.

08 / 01

At last, all the previous ones mastered as one.

In August 2026, Valle recorded Insane Baroque Blaze — a baroque piece on the guitar. Weeks later he went to measure his own recording, out of technical curiosity.

He was sure of one thing: that there was no tritone in it at all, the interval historically nicknamed diabolus in musica.

He was wrong, and wrong on the basics.

The tritone lives inside every dominant seventh chord. Whoever writes baroque meets tritones, knowingly or not. The count was never going to be zero. And a caveat is due: there was no universal church decree “banning” the interval; the excommunication story is later folklore.

In the full passes, the two independent engines returned 83, 91 and 80 melodic tritones. A third Basic Pitch pass, tuned to the low register, returned 49. That is why this page does not treat “80 to 90” as a universal range: it gives the four results separately. The first reported occurrence appears at 0:01.5 — C#4 to G4, right on the opening leap.

Then somebody tried to reproduce the count from the published description alone, and could not. Nine variants of the rule were tested; the divergence ran from four to nineteen occurrences depending on the file.

The defect was not in the numbers. It was in their description.

⚠ correction published on 25 August 2026

The report said “an interval of 6 semitones” for the melodic line. The detector had always counted interval class 6 — the tritone at any octave — exactly as the harmonic rule already stated.

Change the phrase and the note count, the melodic tritones, the harmonic tritones and the first occurrence all reproduce the files again.

One wrong word in the description of the method.
The numbers were always right.

The corrected rule

  • Melodic: two consecutive notes with interval class 6; the next note begins within 0.6 s of the end of the previous one.
  • Harmonic: two notes sounding together for at least 30 ms with interval class 6.
  • Control: the bass, by the same method, returns 2 melodic and 0 harmonic.

The file does not force a conclusion.

Paganini never declared any pact about himself. He denied it in writing, twice. The ones who declared were the audience.

The word “devil” did not come from him. It came from people who had no other word for what they were seeing.

Paganini died in 1840. Crowley published Aha! in 1910. Insane Baroque Blaze was released in 2026. There is no document linking the three events.

Questions this page provokes.

Did Nando Valle sell his soul to the devil?

No. And the essay does not claim Paganini did either. Paganini denied the rumours about himself in his own lifetime, in writing. This page is about something else: how a historical reputation was built, which documents survived, and what happens when fact, interpretation and imagination are set side by side without pretending to be the same thing.

Does the site claim a supernatural link between Paganini, Crowley and the music?

No. The link built here is narrative, not historical. Paganini supplies the case; Crowley supplies another language for a few questions — will, experience and silence; the recording supplies the part that can actually be tested.

Why does Aleister Crowley appear here?

Because Aha!, published in 1910, holds three ideas that run through the project in an unusual way: the paradox of seven keys and eight stages; the image of the soul struck by the “plectron of the Will”; and the demand that doctrine be submitted to the test of experiment. None of them proves anything about Paganini or about this music. They frame the question.

Does Insane Baroque Blaze really contain tritones?

Yes — and the bare fact is musically less extraordinary than the word “diabolus” suggests. Four published guitar transcriptions return 83, 91, 49 and 80 melodic tritones, given per file and per engine; the first reported occurrence is at roughly 0:01.5. As a control, the bass line by the same method returns 2 melodic and 0 harmonic. The report carries the musical context, the metric used and the corrections — and the point is that the method, the detector and the files were published so anyone can check.

Was the video made with artificial intelligence?

The original musical performance is Nando Valle on guitar. In the video, a digital clone reproduces that performance from the real recording with the help of artificial intelligence. The image is synthetic; the performance behind it is not.
