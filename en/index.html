On Niccolò Paganini, and on what is left over

Nando Valle

Rock • Classical • Soundtracks • Arrangements

"Everything documented. Nothing declared."

Read the full essay

I

The anomaly

They buried Paganini nine times.
Thirty-six years without a final grave.
All of Europe found it unremarkable.

This is not legend. It is record. Between 1840 and 1876, the violinist's body was buried and dug up nine times.

He died in Nice on 27 May 1840. The bishop refused consecrated ground. They embalmed the man. He spent more than a year in the cellar of the house where he died. Then he ended up in an olive oil factory. He crossed thirty-six years changing address. Only in 1876 did a pope authorise the grave.

Thirty-six years is not paperwork.
It begins with a refusal he made himself.

II

The refusal

In May 1840, knowing him to be ill, the bishop of Nice sent a priest to give him the last rites. Paganini refused. He thought it was too early — that he still had time.

A week later he was dead of an internal haemorrhage, with no voice left to call anyone. Father Caffarelli reported to the bishop that the violinist intended to refuse the sacraments. On the strength of that report, the Church denied him consecrated ground.

A priest reporting a refusal explains the bishop's decision. It does not explain why nobody in all of Europe found the thing strange enough to protest.

What the public thought he was.

III

The name they gave him

They thought he was the devil. The belief is the fact.

At a concert in Vienna, a member of the audience publicly declared he had seen the Devil standing beside him, guiding his bow. Others swore they had seen hooves under the tailcoat where the feet should be. This was not two or three people: it was the common sense of a European audience faced with a technique nobody could explain.

With a reputation like that, a bishop who refuses a grave meets no resistance. He confirms what everyone already believed.

But why did the technique look impossible?
That part is in the medical record.

IV

The body

His physician wrote, in 1831, that Paganini's hand was no larger than anyone else's — but that every part of it was so elastic that it doubled its own reach. He twisted the first phalanges sideways, at a right angle, without effort.

At the time there was no name for that. There was a body doing what no other body did. The public used the word it had.

And the same body charged for it. From 1823 on, Paganini was treated with mercury in enormous doses, combined with opium. He lost every tooth. The jaw rotted from the inside. By July 1839 he had not a single tooth in his mouth and could barely eat — less than a year from the bed in Nice. The metal is still there: modern mass spectrometry analysis of his hair found mercury in the strands.

The hand explained the technique.
The mercury explained the ruin.
Neither had a name in 1840.

Which leaves what Paganini himself
did with the name they gave him.

V

The denial

He denied it. In writing. Twice.

In 1828 he published, in a Prague newspaper, a letter from his own mother, full of God, as documentary proof against the rumour that he was the Devil's son. In 1831, in the Revue musicale, he published another about the Vienna episode, calling it ridiculous.

And he went on arriving in black. Pale, thin, hair dishevelled, long coat, dark carriage, and never a word more than strictly necessary.

He denied it in the record that leaves a document.
He confirmed it in the record that leaves an impression.

If he kept the image up in public,
what did he do when nobody was looking?

VI

The hidden instrument

He played guitar.

Not electric guitar — that would only appear in the 1930s. The six-string guitar all of Europe played in his day. Between 1801 and 1804, Paganini dropped off the map and spent the time studying it. This was no hobby: forty-three Ghiribizzi, thirty-seven sonatas and two dozen more pieces, for solo guitar alone. Almost all of his chamber music was written with the guitar inside it.

And he almost never played guitar in public. He kept it for the closed circle.

For anyone who thinks this is a stretch, the object exists. In 1838 the luthier Vuillaume lent him a guitar by Grobert. Paganini signed his name on the soundboard. When he died, Vuillaume gave the instrument to Hector Berlioz, who signed the other side. The guitar is now at the Musée de la Musique, item E.375, with both signatures.

The violin he took care of in writing. In 1837 he left it by will to the City of Genoa, to be preserved in perpetuity. Il Cannone is still there, at the Palazzo Doria-Tursi. They take it out of the case once a month so that someone can play it, just so the instrument does not fall silent.

He was buried nine times.
The violin never stopped playing.

All of this has been in public archives for decades.
Somebody had to put it side by side.

VII

Who went and read

Nando Valle is a lawyer and a guitarist. He read about the nine burials, found the number strange, and did what one does with a strange case file: he went after the documents. The six chapters above are what he found.

There is a reason he recognised the subject. In 1993 and 1994, between sixteen and eighteen years old, he read several books by Éliphas Lévi and Aleister Crowley. In 1994 he reached the Book of the Law. He says that in that year he used to joke with whoever was nearby that he was a magician. This cannot be verified by third parties, and he does not pretend that it can.

Across the Atlantic, in 1993, Alan Moore turned forty and announced that he had decided to become a magician. Moore took up the worship of Glycon, a serpent god that antiquity itself exposed as a wooden puppet. He knows it is a fraud, and worships it anyway: if the gods live in the imagination, proving the fraud does not undo the god — it only reveals the address.

Valle says he had no idea who Alan Moore was.

Thirty-two years went by.
What stayed was a doubt.

VIII

The keys

He remembered a passage about keys. And he remembered seven or eight, never able to settle which.

He went to check. In March 1910, Crowley published in The Equinox a poem called Aha!. The title page announces it as "the sevenfold mystery of the ineffable love".

Sevenfold. Seven.
And he put eight steps inside it.

It was not carelessness. The opening couplet announces both counts at once:

There are seven keys to the great gate,
Being eight in one and one in eight.
Aha! · The Equinox I (3) · 1910

There are seven. And there are eight. The doubt he carried for thirty-two years was not a failure of memory: it was written into the second line of the verse.

The eighth is not a new key.
It is the seven at once.

IX

The measurement

In August 2026, Valle recorded Insane Baroque Blaze — a baroque piece on the guitar. Weeks later he went to measure his own recording, out of technical curiosity. He was certain of one thing: that there was no tritone in it at all, the interval the Middle Ages nicknamed diabolus in musica.

He was wrong, and wrong about the basics. The tritone lives inside every dominant seventh chord. Whoever writes baroque writes tritones, knowingly or not. The count was never going to be zero.

Two independent transcription engines found the same thing: between eighty and ninety tritones in the guitar line. The first at one and a half seconds, right on the opening leap.

Then the report itself was audited, and the melodic count would not reproduce. The defect was not in the numbers — it was in their description: the report said an interval of 6 semitones where the program counts interval class 6, the tritone at any octave. Change the phrase and everything lines up.

One wrong word in the description of the method.
The numbers were right all along.

He was wrong about the zero, and the method about itself.
Both corrections are in the report — with the detector and the files, you can redo the count.

X

The distance

Paganini never declared any pact about himself. He denied it in writing, twice. The ones who declared were the audience.

The word "devil" did not come from him. It came from people who had no other word for what they were seeing.

What is left, after all the documents, is the same thing that was left of Paganini: a technique some could not explain, and a second instrument almost nobody saw. The rest is projection.

The archive is open.
Measure it. Deny it. Name it.
